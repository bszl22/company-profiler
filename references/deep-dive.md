# Deep dive sections

Use these when the user asks for a "full profile" or "deep dive", or says they are preparing for an interview. Produce the standard quick brief first, starting with the snapshot table (add "Leadership" and, for public companies, "Financials" rows to it), then add the sections below after "Recent news" and before "Confidence and gaps". Skip any section that would only contain guesses, and say so in "Confidence and gaps".

The deep dive can run to 2-3 pages. Stay skimmable: short bullets, the most important point first in each section.

## Leadership
Founders and key executives (CEO, CTO, heads of product, sales and GTM), with one line each on prior background. Note any recent leadership changes, since turnover is informative.

## Funding history
A short table of rounds: date, round, amount, lead and notable investors. Add the latest valuation only if it has been reported by a credible source, and label it as reported.

## Pricing and unit economics signals
Published pricing tiers, typical contract size if disclosed, and any public statements on ARR, growth, margins, net revenue retention or profitability. Label anything that is an estimate from a third party.

## Technology and AI
What the product is built on where it is visible (public engineering blog, job descriptions, docs), which AI models or techniques it uses, whether AI is proprietary or built on third-party models, and how much of the product's value depends on it.

## Competitive landscape and moat
Wider set of competitors (direct, adjacent, and in-house alternatives customers might choose). What the company claims as its advantage, and whether the evidence supports it: data advantages, integrations, switching costs, brand, distribution.

## Hiring signals
What the open roles say about where the company is investing (for example a wave of solutions engineers suggests enterprise or complex deployments; sales hires in a new region suggest expansion). List a few representative roles with their teams and locations. Note total openings and how long roles have been open if visible.

## Employee and culture signals
Themes from employer-review sites and public posts. Treat this as directional rather than fact, and cite the source and sample size where visible.

## Risks and open questions
Two to five honest risks (concentrated customers, heavy competition from a large platform, runway concerns, regulatory exposure, dependence on one model provider), then a short list of factual gaps the research could not close (for example current headcount or net retention). State these as gaps, not as questions to put to anyone; the profile stops at understanding the business.
